# Neck Pain and “Tech Neck”: A Desk Worker's Guide to Getting Better

> **Written and reviewed by the BPR clinical team. Last reviewed: 30 July 2026.** This article is for education and is not a substitute for individual assessment, diagnosis or treatment by a qualified healthcare professional.

By the end of a long day at the screen, your neck and shoulders are tight, achy and stiff, and tilting your head feels like hard work. With so many of us now working from home or at a desk for hours on end, this pattern — often nicknamed “tech neck” — has become one of the most common complaints around. The good news is that it's rarely serious, and it responds well to a few practical changes.

## What is “tech neck”?

Neck pain of this kind usually comes from the muscles and joints of the neck becoming irritated and overworked. “Tech neck” is simply a modern label for neck and upper-back pain linked to long periods at screens and devices. It's extremely common and, in the vast majority of cases, not a sign of any serious damage.

One myth is worth tackling head-on. You've probably been told it's all about “bad posture.” Posture plays a part, but the bigger issue is usually staying in one position for too long, combined with low activity and stress. There isn't a single perfect posture to hold all day — the healthiest posture tends to be your next one.

## Why does it happen?

Sustained static positions, long hours of screen time, low overall activity and stress all feed into it. It's no surprise that office and computer workers report some of the highest rates of neck pain of any occupation, and that the shift to home and hybrid working has made it more common still. Notice the theme: it's less about a moment of “bad” posture and more about a lot of stillness — muscles that hold one position for hours simply fatigue and complain.

## What does it feel like?

Typically it's an ache and stiffness across the base of the neck and the tops of the shoulders, with reduced movement and sometimes a headache spreading from the base of the skull. It tends to build over a working day and ease with movement, rest and sleep.

## How it's assessed

We'll look at how your neck moves, examine the muscles and joints, and ask about your work setup and daily habits. Importantly, we screen for the small number of features that need closer attention — pain, pins and needles or weakness spreading into the arm, symptoms after a significant injury, or dizziness — so we can reassure you where reassurance is due and act quickly where it isn't.

## What the evidence says

- Exercise helps. A systematic review and meta-analysis of workplace interventions found that neck and shoulder strengthening exercise reduces neck pain in office workers (Chen et al., 2018).
- Active care is recommended. The clinical practice guideline for neck pain supports exercise, and manual therapy where useful, as part of an active approach rather than rest (Blanpied et al., 2017).
- Movement beats a single 'perfect posture'. The most practical, evidence-aligned message is to move often and build some resilience, rather than trying to hold one 'correct' position all day.

## How it's treated

The evidence points clearly towards an active approach: exercise, regular movement and sensible changes to your work setup (Blanpied et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018). Strengthening and endurance exercises for the neck and shoulder muscles are among the most effective things you can do, and simply breaking up long spells of sitting — moving every 20 to 30 minutes — makes a real difference.

Hands-on treatment can ease symptoms and help you move more freely, and it works best alongside exercise rather than on its own. Sorting out your workstation — screen height, chair support, keyboard position — removes some of the sustained load, and managing stress and sleep matters too, because both influence how much muscle tension you carry.

## What you can do yourself

Move often — set a reminder to change position or stand up every 20 to 30 minutes. Do regular neck and shoulder strengthening and mobility exercises. Set your workstation up so the screen is at eye level and your arms are supported, and pay attention to sleep and stress, which quietly amplify neck tension.

## When to seek help

> **When to seek help.** Seek prompt medical attention for pain, pins and needles or weakness travelling down the arm; neck pain after a significant injury or fall; problems with balance, walking, swallowing or speech; or dizziness with visual disturbance. Otherwise, see a clinician if your neck pain is persistent, keeps returning, or isn't improving with these changes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a single correct posture I should hold?**

No. The evidence doesn't support one 'perfect' posture. What helps most is varying your position and moving regularly — the best posture is your next one.

**How often should I take a break from my desk?**

Aim to change position or stand up roughly every 20–30 minutes. Short, frequent movement breaks beat one long stretch of sitting.

**Are standing desks better?**

They can help by letting you alternate between sitting and standing, but the key benefit is the movement and variety, not standing all day.

**Can neck problems cause headaches?**

Yes. Tension and stiffness in the neck can refer into a headache spreading from the base of the skull — often called a cervicogenic headache.

**When should I be concerned?**

Pain, tingling or weakness spreading into the arm, symptoms after a significant injury, or any dizziness, balance or speech problems all warrant prompt review.
