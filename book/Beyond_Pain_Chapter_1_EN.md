# Beyond Pain
### The science and soul of healing — body, soul and spirit

## Chapter 1 — Pain From the Inside

Two workmen. Two nails. Two opposite fates — and, between them, a lesson that turns almost everything we think we know about pain on its head.

The first case was published in one of the world's most respected medical journals, the *British Medical Journal*, in 1995. A 29-year-old builder arrived at A&E after jumping down onto a 15-centimetre nail that went straight through the sole of his boot. The slightest movement of the nail was unbearable. The pain was so severe that doctors had to sedate him with fentanyl and midazolam — powerful drugs — before they could even touch the wound. Only then did they draw the nail out from below. And when they removed the boot, something that looked like a miracle appeared: the nail had passed *between his toes*. The foot was untouched. Not a scratch, not a drop of blood (Fisher, Hassan and O'Connor, 1995).

Now hold that image, and set beside it the opposite story. A man was using a nail gun when it misfired near his face. He felt a thud, a mild toothache, a bruise on his jaw — and got on with life. He ate, slept, worked. Six days later he went to the dentist about the "toothache". The X-ray revealed a 10-centimetre nail *buried inside his head*, driven through his skull.

One man in agony over a nail that never touched him. Another almost pain-free with a nail lodged in his brain. How can that be?

The answer is the key to this whole book, so it is worth saying slowly:

**Pain is not the damage. Pain is your brain's interpretation of the damage.**

We grow up believing that pain is a faithful report from the body: the more it hurts, the worse the injury. It is intuitive — and it is wrong. Pain is not measured at the injured spot. It is **produced by the brain**, after it weighs one essential question: *"how much danger am I in?"* In the first workman, his eyes saw a nail through his boot, his colleagues shouted, everything screamed "catastrophe" — and the brain produced real, devastating pain, with no injury at all. In the second, nothing said "danger" — and the brain stayed quiet, despite terrible damage.

Notice this carefully: in both cases the pain (or its absence) was **real**. Nobody was pretending. The point is not that pain is "made up". The point is that pain is a **decision** the nervous system makes about how much you need to protect yourself — not an automatic readout of the harm done (Moseley and Butler, 2015).

To understand how that decision is made, let us do what this chapter's title promises: go inside. And in there, pain has a smell, an electric charge and a chemistry all of its own.

### The soup of danger: the chemistry of a pain that begins

Scattered through your skin, muscles, joints and organs are millions of nerve endings called **nociceptors**. The name comes from the Latin *nocere*, "to harm". And here is the first surprise: they are not "pain receptors". They are **danger detectors**. They do not feel pain — they raise the alarm.

What makes a nociceptor fire? Often, pure chemistry. When tissue is injured — a sprain, a burn, a cut — the broken cells and the immune system pour out a genuine **chemical cocktail**: prostaglandins, bradykinin, substance P, histamine, hydrogen ions (acidity) and ATP. Scientists nicknamed this brew the "inflammatory soup". It does two things. First, it switches the sentinels on. Second — and this is what explains why an injured area becomes tender to the lightest touch — it **lowers the threshold** of the nociceptors, leaving them hair-triggered, ready to fire at almost nothing. That is why the skin around a burn stings at a puff of air. It is not your imagination; it is the local chemistry turning up the sensors.

Remember that word: **sensitisation**. It will return, in ever deeper forms, throughout this book.

### The master switch of pain

When a sentinel decides to fire, it has to turn the threat into electricity — a nerve impulse. And how does a nerve, made of flesh, "produce electricity"? By opening tiny gates in its membrane through which charged sodium particles rush in. That flood of sodium is the electrical impulse itself.

One of these gates has a name: **Nav1.7**, a sodium channel found precisely in the danger neurons. And it is so central that it deserves a story.

In the early 2000s, doctors in northern Pakistan were puzzled by a boy who performed on the streets as a kind of pain artist: he pushed knives through his own arms and walked over hot coals — feeling absolutely nothing. He was not brave; he was **unable to feel pain**. Researchers studied his family and two others and found the cause: a mutation that **switches off the Nav1.7 channel**. Without that channel, the sentinel cannot fire, the alarm never leaves, and the person lives in a world without pain (Cox et al., 2006). (The story has a telling, tragic end: the boy is believed to have died leaping from a rooftop on his fourteenth birthday. With no pain to warn him, danger had no voice.)

Why does this matter to you, who simply want relief? Because if there is a chemical switch for pain, it can be **acted upon**. The local anaesthetic your dentist injects works exactly like this: it blocks the sodium channels and the alarm never rises. And laboratories around the world are chasing a painkiller that targets Nav1.7 — the dream of a "pain off-switch" without the burden of opioids. The chemistry that causes pain is also the doorway to quieting it.

### Why chilli burns (and what that teaches about relief)

Try a thought experiment: why does a chilli "burn" your mouth, when it is not hot? Because your nociceptors carry a heat sensor called **TRPV1** — and capsaicin, the compound in chilli, slots into that sensor and fools it. The brain receives the message "I am burning" and produces genuine heat and sting, with no high temperature at all (Caterina et al., 1997). It was such a fundamental discovery that its author, David Julius, was awarded the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 2021.

And here is the useful twist: that same sensor became a treatment. **Capsaicin** creams — yes, the very stuff of chilli — are used for certain kinds of pain. By stimulating TRPV1 in a controlled, repeated way, they deplete the nerve's substance P and, over time, **desensitise** that patch of skin. The chemistry that burns, in the right dose and the right way, can also calm.

### The signal's journey, and the gate that controls the volume

Once fired, the danger message races along the nerves by two "roads". The fast fibres (A-delta) carry the alert at top speed — that is the sharp, immediate pain that snatches your hand off the stove before you have even thought. The slow fibres (C fibres) follow behind, carrying the dull, throbbing pain that lingers. That is why a knock so often arrives in two waves.

The signal climbs to the **spinal cord** and reaches a first checkpoint. It is not a passageway; it is a **gate**. In 1965, two scientists, Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall, published the **gate control theory** in the journal *Science* (Melzack and Wall, 1965). They explained something everybody does without thinking: bump your elbow, and you rub it — and it eases. Why? Because at that gate, pain signals compete for room with touch signals. By rubbing, you flood the gate with touch and **muffle** part of the danger message before it can rise.

And here is the astonishing part: that gate also takes orders from **above** — from the brain itself, which can send messengers down to **open or close** it. Your brain has, quite literally, a volume dial for pain. It is why a soldier wounded in battle, or an athlete mid-match, sometimes only notices the injury later: the brain, busy with something more urgent, kept the gate shut.

Only after passing that gate does the signal reach the brain. And up there is no single "pain centre". There is a **network** — many regions, including those for emotion, memory and meaning. That network compares the alert against everything you are, and only *then* decides whether to produce pain — and how much. So it bears repeating: **pain is not something the body sends to the brain; it is something the brain builds.**

### The pharmacy inside you

There is one more detail most people have never heard: your body makes its own painkillers. When the brain decides to turn the volume down, it releases substances called **endorphins** — chemical cousins of morphine, made by you. It is part of what explains the "runner's high", the strange relief in the middle of a shock, and even a slice of the placebo effect.

And the best part: that inner pharmacy **can be opened**. Movement, laughter, music, prayer, the touch of someone you love, a purpose that pulls you forward — all of these help release your own opioids. You are not merely a passive victim of pain; you carry, inside you, part of the remedy.

### The gift almost nobody wants

If pain is so elaborate — chemical soup, sodium channels, roads, a gate, a whole courtroom in the brain — it is fair to ask: what is it all for? Would a life without pain not be better?

Meet Jo Cameron. Scottish, now in her seventies, she lived most of her life without knowing she was different. Jo feels **almost no pain**. She also feels little fear or anxiety, and heals quickly. It sounds like a blessing — until you hear the details. Jo has burnt her own skin on the cooker and only noticed **by the smell of burning flesh**, because pain never warned her. She discovered severe arthritis in her hip only when doctors were baffled that so damaged a joint did not hurt. She has come through operations that are usually very painful on little more than paracetamol. The cause: rare mutations in the FAAH and FAAH-OUT genes, which keep her pain volume permanently low (Habib et al., 2019; Mikaeili et al., 2023).

Jo's story, and the Pakistani boy's, teaches something our comfort-obsessed culture has forgotten: **pain is a gift.** It is an alarm system that keeps us alive. Those born without it injure themselves unknowingly and tend to die young. The pain you so want to silence is the same pain that has already pulled your hand from the fire and sent you for help in time. It is not your enemy. It is a faithful sentinel.

### So what do we do with pain? (the first solutions)

If you have followed this far, you already hold the first and most powerful tool. Let us gather the solutions that flow from all of it:

**1. Understanding pain already reduces it.** It sounds too good to be true, but it is science. When a person grasps that pain does not equal damage — that the alarm sometimes overreacts — the brain begins to read the signal as less threatening, and the pain genuinely eases. Programmes that teach people how pain works reduce pain and disability in measurable ways (Louw et al., 2016; Moseley and Butler, 2015). This chapter, in a sense, is already treatment.

**2. Close the gate.** Melzack and Wall's theory became an everyday tool: rubbing the spot, applying heat or cold, or using a TENS machine (which sends in electrical "touch") all help to "close the gate" and dampen pain. Simple relief, with real physiology behind it.

**3. Work with the chemistry — wisely.** Anti-inflammatories such as aspirin and ibuprofen work by blocking the production of the prostaglandins in that inflammatory soup — a discovery that won John Vane the Nobel Prize in 1982 (Vane, 1971). They have their place, especially in acute pain. But they have limits: they do not treat the cause, and long-term use exacts a price on the stomach, kidneys and heart. Capsaicin creams and anaesthetic patches (which block sodium) are useful local options for certain pains.

**4. Open the inner pharmacy.** Movement, sleep, connection and purpose release your own endorphins. This is not empty positivity; it is biochemistry.

**5. And the deepest solution of all:** give the brain a sense of **safety**. Because the brain produces pain from the question "how much danger am I in?", anything that lowers the sense of threat — understanding, moving with confidence, sleeping, calming the mind, holding on to hope — turns the volume down at the source. That is what the rest of this book is about, layer by layer.

### The body is a wonder — and that is not merely poetry

Pause for a moment and look at what we have described. Sentinels spread through the whole body. A chemical soup that wakes them. Sodium gates that become electricity. Two roads, one fast, one slow. A gate that opens and closes. A brain that weighs danger, memory, emotion and meaning. And an inner pharmacy of painkillers. All of it in thousandths of a second, unbidden, all the time, to protect you.

Three thousand years ago, a man looked inside himself with no microscope at all and wrote: *"I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made"* (Psalm 139:14). He did not have the names — nociceptor, Nav1.7, endorphin. But he had the right intuition. The body is not a dumb machine that breaks and hurts. It is a piece of engineering whose wisdom still leaves us open-mouthed. And the system that makes you suffer is the same one that keeps you safe.

That changes your relationship with your own pain. It stops being a random punishment and returns to what it always was: **a messenger.** The task is not to hate it or merely to silence it, but to learn to listen to what it came to say — because, as you will see, the message is rarely only about the tissue.

### An honest caveat

I must be clear, so you do not misunderstand me. To say the brain builds pain does **not** mean pain is "imaginary" or "all in your head". Pain is always real. And to say that pain does not always reflect the damage does **not** mean injuries do not matter. A fracture, an infection, certain warning signs still demand real — and sometimes urgent — care. What changes is this: pain is not a reliable ruler of harm. It is a protector that sometimes over-calls the alarm, and sometimes fails to sound it when it should. Understanding that does not diminish your pain. It frees you to care for it the right way.

### What comes next

We have seen how pain is born, how it travels and how it is decided — and why, at heart, it exists for your good. But that raises an uncomfortable question, which may well be yours: *what happens when the alarm simply will not switch off?* When the injury healed long ago, but the pain carries on, month after month, year after year? It is not the body lying. It is something else — and that is where we go next.
